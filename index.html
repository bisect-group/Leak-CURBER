TL;DR

KcatNet estimates how fast an enzyme turns substrate into product when substrate is plentiful. When its training data could include close relatives of the test proteins, it scored 0.49 ().

When we tested it on proteins with sequences unlike those it had trained on, its score fell to 0.13. If you’re working with an unfamiliar protein, that second score tells you more about what to expect.

We integrated records from databases into Leak-CURBER and retrained published models to see how their predictions held up on unfamiliar proteins, molecules and reactions.

Read the full abstract

We present Leak-CURBER, the largest multimodal, leakage-controlled benchmark for evaluating machine learning and deep learning models for enzymatic reactions. Leak-CURBER standardizes data from 10 biochemical databases and curates datasets with multimodal inputs, metrics, and leakage-controlled splits for 4 tasks. The benchmark comprises 10 subtasks: prediction of reaction outcomes (2), enzyme function (2), kinetic parameters (3), and protein–ligand binding (3). Leak-CURBER establishes new evaluation protocols that control for train/validation/test overlap across protein sequences and structures, molecule strings and structures, and reaction similarity, thereby exposing generalization failures of learning methods. Evaluating the state-of-the-art (SOTA) models on Leak-CURBER shows that their strong performance degrades sharply under these splits, often approaching random classification or retrieval performance, and near-zero regression performance. Leak-CURBER includes precomputed embeddings, continual benchmark releases, reproducible curation scripts, and a public leaderboard to support reliable comparison of enzymatic-reaction models. Leak-CURBER is publicly available at https://github.com/bisect-group/Leak-CURBER.

The problem

New to enzymes?

Enzymes

An enzymatic reaction begins in your mouth when you chew bread. Your saliva contains an enzyme called that breaks down starch in the bread, releasing smaller sugars such as maltose. Starch is the for this reaction. Other help us make medicines, food, biofuels and detergents.

Why use machine learning

In a wet lab, researchers test which molecules bind to an enzyme, identify the products of a reaction and measure how fast it runs. Each experiment takes time and materials, so testing every enzyme with every candidate molecule is impractical. Researchers use results from the pairs they have tested to train models and choose which pairs to test next.

Data leakage

Suppose two nearly identical enzymes appear in a dataset. A random can put one in training and the other in the test set. A high score on that test tells us little about a protein from a different family.

Proteins
A similar sequence, or the same fold.
Molecules
The same scaffold, the core ring structure.
Reactions
The same reaction center, the atoms that change.

Data fragmentation

A reaction may have an entry in one database, a kinetic measurement in another and a different protein identifier in each. When researchers link these records independently, differences in the resulting datasets make model scores hard to compare.

More detail: how leakage happens

Two records can share a biochemical clue even when their identifiers differ. Related proteins may have similar binding pockets, molecules may share a scaffold, and reactions may involve the same substrates. Each relationship gives a model a different shortcut.

Source of leakageHow it happens
Protein sequenceA test protein is a close of a training protein. It often shares catalytic residues, domains and binding pockets.
Protein structureA test protein has the same fold or active-site shape as a training protein, even when the sequence identity is low.
Molecule and reactionTest molecules or reactions share scaffolds or reaction centers with training ones, even when the strings differ.
Molecule 3D shapeTest molecules lie close to training molecules in a conformer embedding space, even when 2D fingerprints differ.
Annotation timeThe training data holds knowledge that did not exist yet when a test record was made.
Exact entitiesOne sequence, molecule, reaction or assay key fills several rows. A random split by row puts copies on both sides.

Each task family has its own leakage routes. For kinetics, the same accession, mutants of an enzyme, the same substrate and the same publication can appear on both sides. For binding, they are recurring targets and series. For classification, they are proteins and related annotations. For retrieval, the test reaction can resemble training reactions, and the correct enzyme can be a homolog of a training enzyme.

Researchers have tested models on several kinds of unfamiliar data. For enzyme turnover (kcat), DLKcat split the data at random. TurNuP, CatPred and CataPro tested how well their models predicted turnover for enzymes whose sequences differed from those in training. ColdDTA held out drugs and proteins in binding tests. CARE and ReactZyme used more demanding splits for enzyme classification and retrieval.

The choice of split matters. If we group enzymes by sequence, a test enzyme can still act on a substrate the model has seen. If we group molecules by scaffold, a test protein can still belong to a familiar enzyme family.

How current benchmarks fall short

EnzymeMap and ECREACT focus on reaction modeling. CARE and ReactZyme cover enzyme classification and retrieval. ProteinShake includes a binding affinity task among its protein structure tasks. In this comparison, Leak-CURBER alone includes kinetics and covers all five tasks.

Leakage-controlled splits

Leak-CURBER offers seven ways to split the data. Grouped random (GR) keeps identical keys together, whether the key is a sequence (GR-seq), molecule (GR-SMILES) or reaction (GR-rxnsmi). Time holds out newer records. The five similarity splits hold out proteins by sequence or structure (P-seq, P-struct), molecules by fingerprint or shape (Mol-2D, Mol-3D), or reactions by fingerprint (DRFP).

The seven split types

Grouped-random (GR)

We use GR as the reference for performance on familiar data. Each subtask also has splits that reflect the inputs a model might encounter in a new application.

Rows that share a protein sequence (GR-seq), molecule (GR-SMILES) or reaction SMILES (GR-rxnsmi) go to the same set. A reaction and its reverse form one group. This removes exact repeats but keeps related rows close.

Similarity splits

SplitHow similarity is measured
P-seqProtein sequences. MMseqs2 compares sequence identity. Even the closest training match has low identity to the test protein.
P-structProtein structures. Foldseek detects shared folds even in distantly related proteins. We use an experimental structure when available, followed by AlphaFold, ESMFold2 and ESM3 in that order.
Mol-2D2D molecular structure. We measure between Morgan fingerprints.
Mol-3D3D molecular shape. We compare Uni-Mol2 embeddings of molecular conformers.
DRFPReactions. describe which parts of the molecules change from reactants to products.

For every similarity split, a test entity must fall below the threshold to all other entities. Above-threshold overlap is 0% by construction. The takes the next most dissimilar entities, so it is slightly easier than the test set.

Time

We order records by the protein’s UniProt annotation date. Later records go into validation and test.

More detail: comparison with earlier splitting strategies

Random splits measure interpolation. GR splits remove exact identifier overlap but allow proteins, related folds, scaffold and near-duplicate reactions on both sides.

Cluster-based splits do better. Sequence clusters group homologs, and Butina or BitBIRCH clustering groups similar fingerprints. These methods reduce redundancy, but cluster membership does not show that every pair across clusters is below a threshold.

ProteinShake and Leak-CURBER both use Foldseek, for different goals. ProteinShake builds structure clusters and samples clusters up to the split size. We compute the maximum LDDT of each protein against the whole dataset and fill the test set with the most isolated proteins.

DataSAIL assigns examples to folds to lower the total similarity across folds. We define novelty for each entity instead. No held-out protein has a sequence neighbor above the sequence threshold, and the same holds for structures, molecules and reactions.

Limitations
  • Assay conditionsSome enzymes and organisms have far more measurements than others. Most records omit pH and temperature, which makes measurements from different labs harder to compare.
  • Protein structuresPredicted structures can miss the shape a protein takes when a ligand binds. On P-struct, we cannot tell how much of a score change comes from the source of the structure.
  • Pretraining overlapEven on the DRFP split, some test reactions resemble patent reactions the models saw during pretraining. That overlap can raise reaction prediction scores.

Compare a model on the leaderboard

Each subtask has its own leaderboard and fixed test split. A score of 1 is perfect. A score of 0 matches the reference prediction, such as predicting the training mean. The minimum score is −1.

How the score works

Each subtask has two metrics. Regression uses SCC and MSE on log10 targets. Enzyme classification and retrieval use EC level accuracy at Level 4 and Level 1, counting only the first guess. Forward prediction and retrosynthesis use Top-1 and Top-10 exact match.

A zero-point predictor B sets the zero point. For SCC, B is 0. For MSE, B is the training-mean predictor. For EC level accuracy, B always predicts the most frequent training EC. For Top-k, B is 0. The perfect value \(P_m\) is 1, or 0 for MSE.

Let \(\mathcal{M}_t\) be the two metrics of subtask t, and let m be one of them. The score is

\[ s_{tm} = \max\!\left(\frac{M_{tm} - B_{tm}}{P_m - B_{tm}},\, -1\right) \]

\[ S_t = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}_t|} \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}_t} s_{tm} \]

Here \(M_{tm}\) is the model result and \(B_{tm}\) is the zero-point result. A negative score is worse than the zero-point predictor.
